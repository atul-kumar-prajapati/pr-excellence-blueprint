How to raise great PRs

By the end of this, you can raise a PR with an agent’s help that a human can actually review, and that you would ship on a Friday without a knot in your stomach.

Why this, now

You can raise twenty PRs a week. That is the problem.

Alone, maybe two a week. With an agent, twenty is easy. Java backends in an enterprise do not forgive “it compiled.”

It has to last. Stay fast. Stay compliant. Stay maintainable. Stay secure. Somebody else’s service will call yours, so you document. When you do that, you get claps. That is the whole point of today.

Reveal the second paragraph. Then ask: who here has already felt the volume jump?

A story from our own work

We raised a 5,000-line PR. They said no.

  • We opened it. One PR. About five thousand lines.
  • They refused. “We cannot merge this.” If it went in and failed, you would not even know what broke.
  • Reputation. They said CodeWalnut is supposed to be the small-PR shop. This put that on the line.
  • That night. We split it. One around 2,000 lines. Several around 500. Then they signed off.

A big PR does not save time. It hides the break, and it burns the people who have to merge it.

Tell it in that order. Do not invent ticket 4127. Ask: would you put your name on a 5,000-line approval?

The sentence to remember

A great PR makes the reviewer’s job easy, and is safe to release.

Hit right arrow. We unpack that one piece at a time.

Do not skip ahead. The next two slides are the unpack.

What makes the reviewer’s job easy?

They can finish it in one sitting, without guessing.

You followed the coding standards. Lint is already green. They are not here to argue about tabs.
You ran the tests, and the tests would fail if the change were wrong.
The Why is in the PR. They can see the purpose without opening Jira.
The production change is small enough to read. Tests can be longer than the code they protect. That is fine. A hard line-count on the whole PR is not the rule.

Reveal one card at a time. Size is the one people argue with. Hold it.

What makes it safe to release?

You already thought about the people who live with this on Monday.

Logs and metrics so support can see what happened.
The product owner can tell it actually does what was asked, because you attached test evidence.
If we have to back out, we know how. Often that is just the previous build. Say so when it is more than that.

To know it is safe to release, we have to know who gets hurt. Next we name them.

This is the bridge into stakeholders. Do not say “14 readers.”

The checklist, in English

Habits. Then we open a PR.

1. Title

A stranger knows what changed.

2. Why

The reviewer can see the purpose without opening Jira. A ticket link is not a substitute.

3. Tests

They would fail if you reverted this.

4. Size

A human can review the behaviour change. Tests may be longer. That is not a hard line-count.

5. Non-functionals

Durable, fast enough, compliant, observable.

6. Lint

Already run. Not a review comment.

7. Evidence

Show it, or say you did not run it and why.

The written template is in handouts. We are not collecting slogans. We are filling that list.

Title, in practice

Say what happened. Then say why.

What people write when they are tired
fix discount bug

Fixed the discount issue. Tested locally.
What a friend would actually tell you
fix(pricing): round promo discounts to 2dp
using HALF_EVEN (#231)

Finance saw totals like 212.0749999.
We agreed HALF_EVEN with them.
Risk: medium. Checkout and invoices.
No schema change, no API change.

The risk line is the cheapest upgrade. Ask who reads titles at 3 a.m.

Score it the same way every time

Merge confidence, 1 to 5

5 Merge it.

4 Nits only.

3 Some risk, not blocking. You said so in the PR.

2 Blocking. Change it first.

1 Split it or rethink it.

Security, data, or a missing rollback: cap at 2.

You claimed a test and did not show it: cap at 3.

What we will not drop

Five rules. Three you already heard. Two from the people who get burned.

One purpose per PR

If you need “and” in the title, split it.

No claim without evidence

If you say you tested it, show the test. “Tested locally” is a shrug.

You own what you approve

Read the deleted lines. Do not stamp LGTM on a skim.

Keep it small enough to review

Judge the behaviour change, not a raw line-count. Tests can be longer. Unrelated files make people skim.

Know who gets hurt if this is wrong

Reviewer is one. Product, support, the API consumer, the person on call are others. Cover them in the PR.

Build from the lab first if you have time, then show this. We dropped “be clear and kind” and “make it reversible.”

Agents

Use the agent. You still own every line.

Ask it to draft the title and the Why from the diff. Check the Why stands alone without Jira, and that it did not invent work the ticket never asked for.
Ask it to review against the checklist and suggest a 1 to 5. You keep the number.
It will happily write a test that asserts nothing, or put @Transactional on a private method. That compiles. It is still wrong.
Do not paste secrets or customer data into a prompt. Say in the PR when an agent helped.

Before you go

One thing you will do as an author. One as a reviewer.

Make it specific enough that someone could check next month.

Two silent minutes. Two people share. Push “better descriptions” into something you can see on a PR.

After the room

Take the checklist home. Use it on the next PR, not next quarter.

1 Agree the template. Keep, change, or drop a line.
2 Put it in .github/pull_request_template.md
3 Run the self-review prompt before you ask a human.
4 Look again in a few weeks: PR size, time to first review, rework.

Easy to review. Safe to release. Claps follow that, not the other way around.
