How to raise great PRs

90 minutes. Tech-stack agnostic principles.

Examples: Java backend focused.

Goal: Raise an (AI-assisted) PR that a human can actually review and that you can ship on a Friday with confidence.

1. Why this, now

You used to raise maybe two PRs a week. With an agent, twenty is normal.

Volume went up. The bar did not move.
In an enterprise, especially on Java, “it works” is not a PR.
It has to last. Stay fast. Stay compliant. Stay maintainable. Stay secure. Basically check the relevant NFRs.
Integrations need to hold. Other people will live with it, so you write for them. You do these today and build a reputation gradually.

2. We raised a 5,000-line PR. They said no.

One PR. About five thousand lines.

  • They said: we cannot merge this.
  • If it went in and failed, you would not even know what broke.
  • They reminded us: CodeWalnut is supposed to be the small-PR shop. Reputation on the line.
  • We split it that night. One around 2,000 lines. Several around 500. Then they signed off.

A big PR does not save time. It hides the break, and it burns the people who have to merge it.

3. The sentence to remember

A great PR makes the reviewer’s job easy, and is safe to release.

Hit right arrow. We unpack that one piece at a time.

What makes the reviewer’s job easy?

They can finish it without guessing.

You followed the coding standards. Lint is already green. They are not here to argue about tabs.
You ran the tests, and the tests would fail if the change were wrong.
The Why is in the PR. They can see the purpose without opening Jira.
The production change is small enough to read. Tests can be longer than the code they protect. That is fine. A hard line-count on the whole PR is not the rule. Past a sitting’s worth of behavior change, people skim.

What makes it safe to release?

You already thought about Monday.

Logs and metrics so support can see what happened.
The product owner can tell it actually does what was asked, because you attached test evidence.
If we have to back out, we know how. Often that is just the previous build. Say so when it is more than that.

To know it is safe to release, we have to know who gets hurt and the blast radius.

4. Who is this PR for

The usual answer is “the reviewer.” That is not accurate in one sense.

Not everyone reads your PR. A customer never opens GitHub. They feel the total on the ticket.

But there are different stakeholders, at different levels. Each person: why they care, and what they need from you.

5. Habits, then we open a PR

Fill this list. Then open the lab.

Title

A stranger knows what changed.

Why

The reviewer can see the purpose without opening Jira. A ticket link is not a substitute.

Tests

They would fail if you reverted this.

Size

A human can review the behavior change. Tests may be longer. That is not a reason to skip them, or to treat a line-count as a hard cap.

Non-functionals

Durable, fast enough, compliant, observable.

Lint

Already run. Not a review comment.

Evidence

Show it, or say you did not run it, with a justified reason.

Title, in practice · Why, in practice

Say what happened. Then say why, without sending them to Jira.

Title, tired: “fix discount bug. Tested locally.”

Title, useful: fix(checkout): round promo discounts to 2dp using HALF_EVEN (use Conventional Commits)

Why, tired: “See Jira.”

Why, useful: Product wants Friday 15% off checkout. Finance agreed 15% of the current booking total. This PR applies that at booking time.

How ready is this PR to merge?

Pick 1 to 5.

5 Merge now.

4 Small comments only. Safe after those.

3 Some risk. You wrote it in the PR. Not a blocker.

2 Must fix before merge.

1 Too big or the approach is wrong. Split it.

7. Five rules we will not drop

Hold these after the lab.

One purpose per PR

If you need “and” in the title, split it.

No claim without evidence

If you say you tested it, show the test. “Tested locally” should not be the norm.

You own what you approve

Read the deleted lines. Do not stamp LGTM on a skim.

Keep it small enough to review

Judge the behavior change, not a raw line-count. Tests can be longer. If they need twenty minutes of scrolling through mixed unrelated files, they will skim.

Know who gets hurt if this is wrong

Reviewer is one. Product, support, the API consumer, the person on call are others. Cover them in the PR.

8. Agents

Use the agent. You still own every line.

Ask it to draft the title and the Why from the diff. Check the Why stands alone without Jira, and that it did not invent work the ticket never asked for.
Ask it to review against the checklist and suggest a 1 to 5. You keep the number.
It will happily write a test that asserts nothing, or put @Transactional on a private method. That compiles. It is still wrong.
Do not paste secrets or customer data into a prompt. Say in the PR when an agent helped.

9. Before you go

One thing you will do as an author. One as a reviewer.

Specific enough that someone could check next month.

Take this home

Agree the template. Use it on the next PR.

Put it in .github/pull_request_template.md

Look again in a few weeks: PR size, time to first review, rework.

Session outcome: Easy to review. Safe to release.
